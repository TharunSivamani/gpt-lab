PART 02Gradient checkby hand, in one pass

loss.backward() is a black box. If it's broken, training is broken — silently. Check it the way physicists check calculators: pick one weight, change it a tiny bit, measure the loss change, compare.

Procedure, step by step

2Loss at w+ε

3Loss at w−ε

4(L⁺ − L⁻) / 2ε
→
5Compare to backward()

The math

# Two ways to estimate dL/dW at one element of W
analytic  :  PyTorch autograd's chain rule
numerical :  ( L(W + ε)  −  L(W − ε) ) / (2 ε)     ← central difference

Both should agree. If they don't, autograd is broken.
// why ε = 0.01 and not the textbook 1e-5?

Float32 has ~7 decimal digits. Cross-entropy aggregates across every token. A nudge of 1e-5 produces a loss change that rounds to zero. ε = 0.01 makes the loss visibly move at the cost of O(ε²) ≈ 1e-4 truncation bias. Compare relative error, not absolute.

an element of the MLP output projection of the first transformer block
Three sub-tests
[0,0]one scalar · ε=0.01
[1,2]different scalar · same ε

If the check fails

1
Forgot model.zero_grad() — your .grad is from a previous step
2
Forgot loss.backward() — you're reading a stale .grad
3
An in-place op (e.g. x.add_(y)) broke the autograd graph
4
Parameter is an int or non-floating buffer — autograd silently skips it
5
Wrapped the wrong region in torch.no_grad()
// lesson

These are exactly the bugs the loss curve won't catch. Costs ~10 ms to check. Run it before you trust any training run.

PART 03Broken accumulationavg-of-avgs is a lie

Training processes data in micro-batches — small chunks accumulated into one logical step. If micro-batches all have the same number of tokens, averaging the losses is correct. If they don't (variable-length sequences, packing) — it's wrong. Every small batch gets equal say, even though it carries fewer tokens.

✓ correct · weight by tokens
L = (Σᵢ Lᵢ · nᵢ) / Σᵢ nᵢ
Every token gets one vote.
✗ broken · average of averages
L = (1/K) · Σᵢ Lᵢ
Every micro-batch gets one vote regardless of size.

What it looks like over 80 accumulation steps

How to read this: each x-axis point is one accumulation step (4 micro-batches, token counts 8–512). The two lines track each other closely — that's the trap. The broken estimator is biased but the bias is small per step. Hover the chart for exact values at any step.

correct (weighted) broken (avg of avgs) visually close = systematic bias, not noise

What this proves: the broken estimator systematically under-represents large batches. ~5–15 % extra training time to hit the same target loss. F.cross_entropy(reduction='sum') divided by total tokens is the canonical fix.

// lesson

Fix is one line. Replace mean(losses) with sum(L·n) / sum(n).

PART 05Honest MFUhow fast are we really?

MFU = (achieved FLOPs / step) / (GPU peak FLOPs). A100 at 40 % is ~125 TFLOPS. On a 3060 laptop (FP32 peak ~13, BF16 dense ~26 TFLOPS) the ceiling is lower and harder to hit because the chip has only 30 SMs. Let's measure honestly.

Measured
MFU vs peak
40 % "well-tuned" target

What's costing us the distance to 40 %?

Hardware ceiling
30 SMs vs 108 on an A100 — 3.6× less compute parallelism per launch.
Tensor cores are idle
Loop runs in plain FP32; BF16 tensor cores never engage. Loss is FP32.
Workload shape
Small batch (B=16, T=64) — GEMMs don't fill the SMs. CPU dispatch overhead matters.
Optimiser overhead
No fused optimiser — extra kernel launch per group. Use AdamW(fused=True).

How to actually close the gap (in order of effort)

1
Cast the matmuls to BF16 — the big one on a 30-SM chip. Tensor cores are ~2× faster.
2
Larger batch — keep the SMs busy.
3
Use torch.compile — fuses elementwise ops, cuts launch overhead.
4
Fused optimiser — AdamW(fused=True) removes a kernel launch.
// what's NOT costing us

The model being too small. FLOPs are real — kernel-launch overhead is just amortised over too few FLOPs at this size.

The verdict

// what I would train in, and why

BF16. It keeps FP32's exponent range (±3.4 × 10³⁸) so loss spikes don't saturate and gradients don't underflow. It only loses 3 bits of mantissa vs FP16 — that matters when accumulating dot products over thousands of elements. Canonical recipe: BF16 forward + backward, FP32 master weights + optimiser state.

FP8 is a throughput format, not a precision upgrade. Its tiny range (~448) means it needs per-tensor scaling factors and an FP32 master copy of the weights. On the 3060 we don't yet need the extra 2× — BF16 is the right training precision.

FINALThe recapsix habits, in order

1
Print the shapes. Shape errors are loud — PyTorch throws a stack trace. Loss errors are silent — broadcasted shapes can train for hours. Print every intermediate tensor before you trust a loss.
2
Check one gradient. Perturb one weight, measure the loss, compare to backward(). Catches missing zero_grad, in-place ops, wrong dtype. ~10 ms.
3
Be aware of your accumulation math. When micro-batches have different sizes, mean(losses) lies. Weight by token count: sum(L·n) / sum(n).
4
Watch the grad norm. The loss curve lags; the grad norm is the leading indicator. If only one is moving, that's information about what's actually wrong.
5
Report MFU honestly. If you don't know what fraction of peak you're hitting, you don't know whether to spend your time on the model or on the harness. Measure it.
6
Respect the format. BF16 for forward/backward, FP32 for master weights and optimiser state. FP8 is a throughput trick, not a precision upgrade.
